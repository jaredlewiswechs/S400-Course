## Unit Narrative

Geometry begins with the question: How do you know something is true? In arithmetic, you can check an answer. In geometry, you need to reason. This unit establishes the foundations: undefined terms (point, line, plane), definitions, postulates, and the structure of logical argument. Students learn that geometry is a system built on agreed-upon starting points — much like a legal system or a building code. The connection to architecture and engineering runs throughout: building codes are logical constraints ("if the span exceeds 12 feet, then a support beam is required"), and compass-and-straightedge constructions are the original precision tools. Students construct perpendicular bisectors, angle bisectors, and parallel lines — first physically, then in GeoGebra — and justify why each construction works. By the end of the unit, students can write conditional statements, identify their converses, and construct basic logical arguments.

## Lesson Sequence

### Week 1: The Language of Geometry (≈8.3 hours)

#### Lesson 1.1 — Points, Lines, and Planes: The Building Blocks (2 hrs)
**Geometry Focus:** Undefined terms, notation, basic definitions
**Activity:**
- Warm-up: Show a photograph of a building under construction. Ask: "What geometric shapes do you see? What are the simplest geometric elements?"
- Direct instruction: Geometry starts with three undefined terms — point (location, no size), line (extends infinitely, no width), and plane (flat surface, extends infinitely).
- We don't define these; we describe them. Everything else is built from them.
- Defined terms:
  - Segment: Part of a line with two endpoints. Notation: `AB` with a bar.
  - Ray: Part of a line with one endpoint. Notation: `AB` with an arrow.
  - Angle: Two rays sharing an endpoint (vertex). Notation: `∠ABC` or `∠B`.
  - Collinear points: Points on the same line. Coplanar points: Points on the same plane.
- Notation practice: Name points, lines, segments, rays, and angles from a diagram.
- Architecture connection: A steel beam is a segment. The edge of a wall is a line. A floor is a plane. An intersection of two walls is a line. The corner where two walls and a floor meet is a point.
- GeoGebra: Construct points, lines through two points, segments, rays. Measure lengths and angles.

**TEKS:** §111.41(c)(1)

#### Lesson 1.2 — Segments, Angles, and Measurement (2 hrs)
**Geometry Focus:** Measuring segments and angles, classification
**Activity:**
- Segment measurement: Use a ruler to measure segments to the nearest millimeter. In GeoGebra, measure precisely.
- Segment Addition Postulate: If B is between A and C, then `AB + BC = AC`. Practice: Given `AB = 5` and `BC = 8`, find `AC`.
- Angle measurement: Use a protractor. In GeoGebra, use the angle tool.
- Angle classification: Acute (< 90°), right (= 90°), obtuse (> 90°, < 180°), straight (= 180°).
- Angle Addition Postulate: If ray BD is in the interior of ∠ABC, then `m∠ABD + m∠DBC = m∠ABC`.
- Special angle pairs:
  - Complementary: Sum = 90°.
  - Supplementary: Sum = 180°.
  - Vertical angles: Formed by intersecting lines, always equal.
- Architecture connection: A right angle is the foundation of construction — walls must be perpendicular to floors. A protractor is an ancient tool that builders still use.
- Practice: 15 problems — find missing segment lengths and angle measures using addition postulates and special pairs.

**TEKS:** §111.41(c)(1)

#### Lesson 1.3 — Conditional Statements: The Grammar of Proof (2 hrs)
**Geometry Focus:** If-then statements, hypothesis, conclusion
**Activity:**
- A conditional statement has the form: "If P, then Q." P = hypothesis, Q = conclusion.
- Geometric examples:
  - "If two angles are vertical angles, then they are congruent."
  - "If a point is on the perpendicular bisector of a segment, then it is equidistant from the endpoints."
- Non-geometric examples (to build intuition):
  - "If it is raining, then the ground is wet." (True)
  - Converse: "If the ground is wet, then it is raining." (Not necessarily true — sprinklers!)
- Define:
  - Converse: Swap hypothesis and conclusion. "If Q, then P."
  - Inverse: Negate both. "If not P, then not Q."
  - Contrapositive: Swap and negate. "If not Q, then not P." (Always has the same truth value as the original.)
- Building code connection: "If the building exceeds 3 stories, then a fire escape is required." Write the converse, inverse, and contrapositive. Which are true?
- Biconditional: "P if and only if Q" — true when P and Q have the same truth value.
- Practice: 10 conditional statements — write converse, inverse, contrapositive, and determine truth values.

**TEKS:** §111.41(c)(4)

#### Lesson 1.4 — Inductive vs. Deductive Reasoning (2.3 hrs)
**Geometry Focus:** Two types of reasoning, counterexamples
**Activity:**
- Inductive reasoning: Observe specific cases → make a general conjecture.
  - Look at angles of triangles: 60+60+60=180, 90+45+45=180, 70+60+50=180. Conjecture: "The sum of angles in a triangle is 180°." (True — but inductive reasoning alone doesn't prove it.)
- Deductive reasoning: Start with known facts (postulates, definitions, proven theorems) → reach a definite conclusion.
  - "All right angles are 90°. ∠A is a right angle. Therefore, ∠A = 90°." (This is certain.)
- Counterexample: To disprove a conjecture, find ONE counterexample.
  - "All prime numbers are odd." Counterexample: 2 is prime and even.
  - "If a shape has 4 sides, then it is a rectangle." Counterexample: A parallelogram that is not a rectangle.
- GeoGebra exploration: Students test 5 geometric conjectures by constructing examples. For each, determine whether the conjecture appears true or find a counterexample.
- Architecture connection: An architect uses deductive reasoning when calculating load-bearing capacity. They can't test every possible weight — they use physics formulas (general rules) to deduce the answer for their specific building.
- Practice: 8 reasoning problems — classify as inductive or deductive; find counterexamples for false conjectures.

**TEKS:** §111.41(c)(4)

### Week 2: Constructions — The Original Precision Tools (≈8.3 hours)

#### Lesson 1.5 — Compass and Straightedge: Copy a Segment and an Angle (2 hrs)
**Geometry Focus:** Basic constructions with compass and straightedge
**Activity:**
- Historical context: The ancient Greeks (Euclid, ~300 BC) established that all geometric constructions should use only a compass (for circles) and a straightedge (for lines) — no measuring. These constraints forced precise reasoning about WHY constructions work, not just HOW.
- Construction 1: Copy a segment.
  1. Draw a ray. Place the compass on the original segment to capture its length. Transfer to the ray.
  2. Why it works: The compass preserves distance. The new segment has the same length as the original.
- Construction 2: Copy an angle.
  1. Draw a ray. Create arcs on the original angle and the new ray. Transfer the opening with compass.
  2. Why it works: Congruent triangles (preview — students write an intuitive justification now, formal proof in Unit 3).
- Physical practice: Students perform each construction with compass and straightedge on paper (3 attempts each).
- GeoGebra: Recreate the same constructions using the compass and line tools. Compare to the physical version.
- Key principle: Every construction must be justified — not just "it works" but "here's why it must work."

**TEKS:** §111.41(c)(4)

#### Lesson 1.6 — Perpendicular Bisector and Angle Bisector (2.5 hrs)
**Geometry Focus:** Bisector constructions and their properties
**Activity:**
- Construction 3: Perpendicular bisector of a segment.
  1. Open compass to more than half the segment length. Draw arcs from both endpoints. Connect the intersection points.
  2. Result: A line perpendicular to the segment at its midpoint.
  3. Why it works: The intersection points are equidistant from both endpoints (by compass radius). Any point equidistant from both endpoints lies on the perpendicular bisector.
- Construction 4: Angle bisector.
  1. From the vertex, draw an arc crossing both rays. From those intersection points, draw equal arcs. Connect the vertex to the new intersection.
  2. Result: A ray that divides the angle into two congruent angles.
  3. Why it works: Symmetric construction creates congruent triangles on each side.
- Architecture connection: Perpendicular bisectors appear in finding the center of a circular arch. Angle bisectors appear in corner trim and molding cuts — a miter saw cuts at the angle bisector.
- Practice: 3 constructions of each type (physical + GeoGebra). Verify by measuring.
- GeoGebra investigation: Construct the perpendicular bisectors of all three sides of a triangle. They meet at a single point (the circumcenter). Why?

**TEKS:** §111.41(c)(4)

#### Lesson 1.7 — Constructing Perpendicular and Parallel Lines (2 hrs)
**Geometry Focus:** Perpendicular through a point, parallel through a point
**Activity:**
- Construction 5: Perpendicular to a line through a point on the line.
  1. From the point, draw equal arcs on both sides. From those points, draw arcs above (or below). Connect.
- Construction 6: Perpendicular to a line through a point NOT on the line.
  1. From the point, draw an arc crossing the line at two places. Construct the perpendicular bisector of those two crossing points.
- Construction 7: Parallel to a line through a point not on the line.
  1. Draw a transversal through the point and the line. Copy the angle formed at the line to the point. The new ray is parallel.
  2. Why: Corresponding angles are congruent → lines are parallel (preview of Unit 2).
- Architecture connection: Every wall perpendicular to the floor, every floor parallel to the one below it — these are constructions builders perform with levels and plumb lines.
- Practice: Physical constructions + GeoGebra verification.

**TEKS:** §111.41(c)(4)

#### Lesson 1.8 — Construction Challenges (1.8 hrs)
**Geometry Focus:** Applying constructions to solve problems
**Activity:**
- Challenge problems:
  1. Construct an equilateral triangle (all sides equal — use compass to draw three equal segments).
  2. Construct a square (perpendicular lines + equal segments).
  3. Inscribe a regular hexagon in a circle (radius = side length of hexagon).
  4. Find the center of a given circle using only compass and straightedge (construct two chords, find their perpendicular bisectors — they intersect at the center).
- Architectural application: Given a triangular plot of land, construct the circumscribed circle (circumcenter) — this is the largest circle that fits around the property with its center equidistant from all three corners.
- GeoGebra: Complete all challenges digitally and save screenshots for the portfolio.

**TEKS:** §111.41(c)(4)

### Week 3: From Reasoning to Proof (≈8.3 hours)

#### Lesson 1.9 — Postulates, Axioms, and Theorems (2 hrs)
**Geometry Focus:** The logical structure of geometry
**Activity:**
- Postulates/axioms: Statements accepted as true without proof. They are the starting rules.
  - "Through any two points, there is exactly one line."
  - "Through any three noncollinear points, there is exactly one plane."
  - "If two lines intersect, then they intersect in exactly one point."
- Theorems: Statements that must be proven using postulates, definitions, and previously proven theorems.
  - "Vertical angles are congruent." (This can be PROVEN from the definition of supplementary angles.)
- The structure of geometry is like the structure of a legal system: axioms are the constitution, theorems are case law derived from the constitution.
- Students list 5 postulates and 5 definitions they've learned so far. They will use these as tools in proofs.
- Architecture connection: Building codes work the same way — base requirements (postulates) and derived requirements (theorems). "If the roof load exceeds X, THEN additional supports are required" is provable from structural engineering axioms.

**TEKS:** §111.41(c)(4)

#### Lesson 1.10 — Writing Two-Column Proofs (2.5 hrs)
**Geometry Focus:** Two-column proof format — statements and reasons
**Activity:**
- A two-column proof has two columns: Statements (what is true) and Reasons (why it's true — definition, postulate, or theorem).
- Example proof: Prove that vertical angles are congruent.
  - Given: Lines l and m intersect at point P, forming ∠1, ∠2, ∠3, ∠4.
  - Prove: ∠1 ≅ ∠3.

| Statement | Reason |
|-----------|--------|
| ∠1 and ∠2 are supplementary | Linear Pair Postulate |
| m∠1 + m∠2 = 180° | Definition of supplementary |
| ∠2 and ∠3 are supplementary | Linear Pair Postulate |
| m∠2 + m∠3 = 180° | Definition of supplementary |
| m∠1 + m∠2 = m∠2 + m∠3 | Substitution (both = 180°) |
| m∠1 = m∠3 | Subtraction Property of Equality |
| ∠1 ≅ ∠3 | Definition of congruent angles |

- Students work through 3 guided proofs with scaffolding (fill in missing statements or reasons).
- Then attempt 2 proofs independently.
- Key message: Proof is not about memorizing steps — it's about building a logical chain from known facts to a conclusion.

**TEKS:** §111.41(c)(4)

#### Lesson 1.11 — Logic & Construction Portfolio Workshop (2 hrs)
**Activity:**
- Students compile their **Logic & Construction Portfolio**:
  1. **8 Constructions** (physical photos or GeoGebra screenshots):
     - Copy a segment, copy an angle, perpendicular bisector, angle bisector, perpendicular through a point (on and off the line), parallel through a point, one challenge construction.
  2. **Justification** for each construction: 2–3 sentences explaining why the construction works using geometric vocabulary.
  3. **3 Conditional Statements** from architecture/building codes: Write each as if-then, identify hypothesis and conclusion, write the converse and determine if it's true.
  4. **1 Two-Column Proof** chosen from the unit, written neatly with all steps.
- Workshop time: organizing, writing justifications, polishing proofs.

#### Lesson 1.12 — Presentations & Unit 1 Assessment (1.8 hrs)
**Activity:**
- Portfolio gallery walk: Students display construction pages and justifications.
- Selected presentations: 5 students present their favorite construction with justification (3 minutes each).
- Unit checkpoint quiz: Vocabulary, angle relationships, conditional statements, basic proof steps (25 minutes).
