# Phase 5 — The Defense

## Phase Overview

| Field | Detail |
|-------|--------|
| Phase | 5 of 6 |
| Title | The Defense |
| Duration | 25 hours (~3 weeks) |
| Driving Question | *Can you defend your findings, your methods, and your recommendations under questioning?* |
| Key Deliverable | Oral defense of the policy brief before an external panel (15 minutes: 8-minute presentation + 7-minute Q&A) |

## Phase Narrative

Writing the brief is one skill. Defending it is another. Phase 5 prepares students for an oral defense modeled on a thesis defense or a policy presentation to a government committee. Students present their findings, explain their methodology, acknowledge limitations, and respond to questions from a panel that includes at least one external evaluator (another teacher, administrator, community member, university professor, or government professional). The defense tests whether students truly understand their own work — not just the output, but the reasoning behind every choice. Can they explain why they chose that test? What would change if the data were different? Why should anyone trust the result? This phase also includes a dry-run presentation and structured preparation for anticipated questions.

## Learning Objectives

- Synthesize a 3,000–5,000 word brief into an 8-minute presentation
- Create effective presentation visuals (not the brief projected — a purpose-built slide deck or visual aid)
- Anticipate and prepare for challenging questions
- Respond to questions with data, reasoning, and intellectual honesty
- Demonstrate command of statistical methodology through oral explanation

## Lesson Sequence

### Week 1: Preparation (≈8.3 hours)

#### Lesson 5.1 — The Art of the Defense (1.5 hrs)
**Activity:**
- What is a defense? Not a performance — a conversation about your work.
- Watch 2 excerpted examples: a thesis defense (abbreviated) and a policy briefing to a committee.
- Key principles:
  1. **Know your story.** You have 8 minutes. What are the 3 things you want the panel to remember?
  2. **Lead with the finding.** Don't bury the conclusion. State it early.
  3. **Show, don't read.** Your visuals supplement your speaking — they are not a script.
  4. **Own your limitations.** The panel will respect honesty more than defensiveness.
  5. **"I don't know" is a valid answer** — followed by "but here's how I would find out."

#### Lesson 5.2 — Building the Presentation (3 hrs)
**Activity:**
- Students create a visual presentation (slides or R Markdown presentation format). NOT the full brief projected on screen.
- Suggested structure (8 minutes, ~10–12 slides):
  1. **Title slide** (5 seconds)
  2. **The problem** (1 minute): One compelling statistic or map. Why does this matter?
  3. **The question** (30 seconds): What did you investigate?
  4. **The data** (1 minute): Where did the data come from? How many observations? Key limitations.
  5. **Key Finding 1** (1.5 minutes): The strongest finding. Show the visualization. State the statistical result.
  6. **Key Finding 2** (1.5 minutes): The second-strongest finding. Show the evidence.
  7. **Key Finding 3 or Robustness** (1 minute): Either a third finding or a robustness check.
  8. **Recommendations** (1 minute): What should be done? Based on which evidence?
  9. **Limitations and Questions** (30 seconds): What can't the data tell us? What remains uncertain?
- Design principles:
  - One idea per slide.
  - Minimal text — bullet points, not paragraphs.
  - Visualizations should be readable from the back of the room.
  - No animations or transitions (they waste time).

#### Lesson 5.3 — Anticipating Questions (2 hrs)
**Activity:**
- The Q&A is where the defense happens. Students prepare for it systematically.
- Generate a list of 10 likely questions:
  1. Why did you choose [that test] instead of [alternative]?
  2. How do you know the relationship is causal and not just correlation?
  3. What are the biggest confounders in your analysis?
  4. If you had more data, what would you do differently?
  5. How confident are you in the [specific finding]?
  6. What does the p-value actually mean in this context?
  7. How would your recommendation change if [finding] turned out to be wrong?
  8. Why did you exclude [specific observations]?
  9. Is [effect size] practically significant or just statistically significant?
  10. Who is the intended audience for your recommendations?
- For each question, students write a 2–3 sentence answer with reference to specific data or results.
- Practice: Students pair up and take turns asking each other 5 questions.

#### Lesson 5.4 — Statistics Explanation Practice (1.8 hrs)
**Activity:**
- The panel will ask about methodology. Can you explain your statistics in plain language?
- Students practice explaining to a non-statistician:
  - "What is a confidence interval?" → "It's a range of plausible values for the true effect. Based on our data, we're 95% confident the real answer falls between ___ and ___."
  - "What is a p-value?" → "It's the probability of seeing results this extreme if there were actually no real effect. Our p-value of 0.003 means there's only a 0.3% chance this result is due to random chance."
  - "What is R²?" → "It's the fraction of the variation in [outcome] that our model explains. R² of 0.65 means our predictors account for about 65% of the differences we see."
  - "What is logistic regression?" → "Instead of predicting a number, it predicts a probability — the likelihood that something happens."
- Pairs practice: One student explains their methodology as if to a city council member. The other plays the council member and asks follow-ups.

### Week 2: Dry Runs (≈8.3 hours)

#### Lesson 5.5 — Dry Run #1: To Classmates (3 hrs)
**Activity:**
- Each student delivers their full 8-minute presentation + 5-minute Q&A to a group of 4–5 classmates.
- Classmate evaluators use a simplified rubric:
  - [ ] I understood the main finding.
  - [ ] The visuals were clear and readable.
  - [ ] The student spoke confidently (not reading from notes).
  - [ ] The student answered questions with data or reasoning.
  - [ ] I know what the student recommends and why.
- Written feedback: 2 strengths, 2 improvements.
- Timing: Teacher enforces the 8-minute limit. If a student goes over, they must cut content.

#### Lesson 5.6 — Revision Based on Dry Run (2 hrs)
**Activity:**
- Students revise their presentations based on dry-run feedback.
- Common fixes: Too much content (cut a finding or merge slides), too fast (practice pacing), visuals too small (increase font/image size), unclear explanations (simplify language).
- Students also revise their Q&A preparation based on the questions they actually received.

#### Lesson 5.7 — Dry Run #2: To the Teacher (3.3 hrs)
**Activity:**
- Each student delivers the full defense to the teacher (or in groups of 3 with the teacher present).
- Teacher asks the hardest questions — the ones the external panel is most likely to ask.
- Teacher provides final feedback:
  - Content: Is the argument clear and well-supported?
  - Delivery: Pacing, eye contact, confidence, handling of questions.
  - Methodology: Can the student explain their statistical choices under pressure?
  - Recommendations: Are they defensible?
- Students have 1 final revision opportunity before the panel defense.

---

### Week 3: The Panel Defense (≈8.3 hours)

#### Lesson 5.8 — Final Preparation (2 hrs)
**Activity:**
- Students do one final practice run independently or with a partner.
- Review Q&A preparation notes.
- Ensure technology works: presentation file, R Markdown knitted, projector compatibility.
- Mental preparation: This is the culmination of 4 courses and 2+ years of work. You know more about your topic than anyone in the room. Trust your analysis.

#### Lesson 5.9 — Panel Defenses (6.3 hrs)
**Activity:**
- Panel composition (minimum):
  - The course teacher (required).
  - At least one external evaluator: another teacher, school administrator, university faculty, community member, government professional, or via video call.
  - Classmates (observing, not scoring).
- Schedule: Each defense is 15 minutes:
  - 8 minutes: Student presentation.
  - 7 minutes: Panel Q&A.
- With a class of 20–25 students, this requires multiple days. Aim for 6–8 defenses per day.
- Scoring (see rubric below): Teacher (50%) + External panelist(s) (50%).
- After each defense: Brief verbal feedback from the panel (2 minutes).

## Defense Rubric (90 points: 50 Presentation + 40 Q&A)

### Presentation (50 points)

| Criterion | Excellent (A) | Proficient (B) | Developing (C) | Beginning (D) | Points |
|-----------|---------------|-----------------|-----------------|----------------|--------|
| **Argument Clarity** | Problem, findings, and recommendations are crystal clear; a policymaker would understand immediately | Mostly clear; minor gaps | Some confusion about the main argument | Unclear or disorganized | 15 |
| **Evidence Quality** | Visualizations are compelling, well-chosen, and directly support the argument; statistics cited accurately | Good evidence with minor issues | Some evidence but not well-integrated | Weak or missing evidence | 15 |
| **Delivery** | Confident, natural, appropriate pace; engages the audience; within time limit | Mostly confident; minor pacing issues | Reads heavily from notes; over/under time | Difficult to follow | 10 |
| **Visual Design** | Clean, professional, readable from back of room; one idea per slide | Mostly clean; minor design issues | Cluttered or hard to read | Unprofessional | 10 |

### Q&A (40 points)

| Criterion | Excellent (A) | Proficient (B) | Developing (C) | Beginning (D) | Points |
|-----------|---------------|-----------------|-----------------|----------------|--------|
| **Methodological Command** | Explains statistical choices clearly; can describe tests in plain language and technical terms | Mostly explains methodology; minor gaps | Struggles with methodology questions | Cannot explain methods | 15 |
| **Intellectual Honesty** | Acknowledges limitations, confounders, and uncertainties openly; "I don't know but I would..." | Acknowledges some limitations | Defensive or dismissive of limitations | Refuses to acknowledge problems | 10 |
| **Data-Grounded Responses** | Cites specific numbers, findings, or analyses in responses | References data generally | Vague responses | Cannot connect to data | 10 |
| **Composure** | Calm, thoughtful, takes time to think; asks for clarification if needed | Mostly composed; occasional nervousness | Visibly uncomfortable; rushes answers | Cannot respond | 5 |
